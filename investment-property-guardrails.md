# Investment Property Analysis Guardrails

> **How to use this document:** This is a structured checklist and knowledge base for evaluating residential investment properties. It is jurisdiction-neutral by design — Finnish-specific examples are noted where relevant, but the principles apply broadly. When analysing a property, work through each section in order and flag any items that match the red/orange flag criteria.

---

## 1. Before Buying

### 1.1 Financial Structure of the Building

- **Capital Charge Treatment (Income vs. Capitalised):**
  Renovation loans held by a building/housing company are repaid via a periodic capital charge levied on unit owners. Understand how this is recorded:
  - **Recorded as Income ("Expensed"):** The charge flows through the company's income statement. As an owner, you can typically deduct the full capital charge (principal + interest) from your taxable rental income each year. This is the investor-preferred treatment.
  - **Capitalised ("Funded"):** The charge is recorded as a capital contribution on the balance sheet. You cannot deduct it from annual rental income. Instead, it increases your acquisition cost, reducing capital gains tax only when you eventually sell. Less favourable for cash-flow investors.
  - **Action:** Confirm which method applies before purchasing. Request the building's property manager certificate or equivalent official document and look for the section describing how capital charges are handled.

- **Extra/Special Maintenance Charges:**
  Check whether additional one-off charges have been levied on owners in recent years, or whether any building-level maintenance loan surcharge is currently active. These reduce net yield and may signal underlying financial stress.
  - **Finnish context — authorised charges:** In Finland it is standard practice for a housing company's articles to authorise the board to levy up to **two** extra maintenance charges per year without a separate shareholder vote. This authorisation alone is not a concern.
  - **Flag if:** (a) any extra charges have actually been levied and collected (used authorisation), or (b) the articles authorise **more than two** extra charges per year. Either situation warrants investigation into the building's financial health.

- **5-Year Maintenance Plan:**
  A forward-looking maintenance plan (typically covering the next 5–10 years) should exist. This document outlines planned repairs and their estimated costs, allowing you to budget for future capital calls.

### 1.2 Legal & Governance

- **Articles of Association / Building Rules:**
  Review the governing document of the building company or body corporate. Specifically check:
  - Are short-term rentals (e.g. Airbnb-style) permitted?
  - Are long-term rentals unrestricted?
  - Are there any usage or alteration restrictions relevant to your strategy?

- **Redemption / Pre-emption Clause:**
  Some building constitutions include a clause allowing existing shareholders or the building company to purchase a unit at the agreed sale price *after* contracts are signed. This means you could complete all due diligence, arrange financing, and pay transfer taxes — only for another party to step in and acquire the property at your price.
  - **Risk level: High.** Avoid unless the deal is exceptional and you have confirmed the risk is acceptable.

### 1.3 Physical Condition

- **Energy Performance Certificate:**
  A valid energy performance certificate must be available. This affects tenant appeal, future rental value, and compliance with evolving energy regulations.

- **Safety Requirements:**
  Confirm that mandatory safety equipment (smoke detectors, carbon monoxide detectors where required, fire extinguishers in applicable jurisdictions) is present and functional. Responsibility for provision typically falls to the landlord; ongoing maintenance may transfer to the tenant.

## 2. When Viewing the Property

### 2.1 Senses

- **Sound:** Can you hear neighbours through walls or ceilings? Is there significant external noise (traffic, industry, entertainment venues)? Noise complaints are a leading cause of tenant dissatisfaction and turnover.
- **Smell:** Does the unit smell fresh? Evidence of prior smoking, dampness, or mould is a serious flag. Mould remediation can be costly and may require the unit to be vacated.

### 2.2 Visible Condition

- **Floors:** Check age and wear of hard floors (parquet, timber, tile). Refinishing or replacement is expensive.
- **Kitchen Surfaces:** Assess countertops, cabinet doors, and appliances for age, damage, and functionality.
- **Bathroom:** Look for signs of moisture damage — discolouration, soft surfaces, tile grout deterioration, or silicone seal failure. Water damage in bathrooms is one of the most expensive repairs.
- **Windows & Doors:** Check for draughts, condensation between panes (failed double-glazing), and ease of operation.
- **Renovations:** Have any renovations been carried out? Were they done with required approvals from the building management? Are there warranties on work or appliances?

## 3. Red & Orange Flags

Use the following framework when reviewing building-level documents. Items marked 🔴 are deal-breakers or require deep investigation. Items marked 🟠 require clarification before proceeding.

### 3.1 Financial Flags

| Flag | Description | Action |
|------|-------------|--------|
| 🔴 **High Building-Level Maintenance Loan** | A large outstanding loan against the building taken to cover operational costs (e.g. heating bills) rather than a planned capital investment. | Ask: *Why was the loan taken?* If for energy upgrades or planned capital works — acceptable. If to cover cash shortfalls — the maintenance fee (service charge) is likely to rise significantly. |
| 🔴 **No Forward Maintenance Plan** | The building has no documented multi-year maintenance plan. Indicates reactive maintenance culture, leading to unpredictable special assessments. | Treat as a major red flag. Request the plan; if unavailable, price in a significant risk premium or walk away. |
| 🔴 **Negative Operating Surplus** | The building's annual financial statement shows a persistent or large negative operating result. The company is spending more than it collects. | Review the last 2–3 years of financial statements. Expect maintenance fee increases. |
| 🔴 **Expiring Land Lease (within 5–10 years)** | The building sits on leased land and the lease is approaching renewal. Rent increases upon renewal can be 300–500%, directly impacting the building's service charge and unit value. | Confirm lease expiry date and renewal terms. Model the impact of a significant rent increase on yield. |
| 🟠 **Leased Land — Optional Purchase** | The land is leased, but shareholders have an option to purchase. Confirm whether your unit's share of any land debt is included in the purchase price, or whether a separate land rent levy applies. | Quantify the cost and timeline of the option. |
| 🟠 **Extra Maintenance Charges Levied or Excessive Authorisation** | In Finland, authorisation to levy up to two extra maintenance charges per year is standard and not a concern. Flag if: (a) any extra charges have actually been collected in recent years, or (b) the articles authorise more than two per year. Either indicates financial pressure or unusual governance. | Check the financial statements for actual extra charges levied. Review the articles of association for the authorised limit. |
| 🟠 **Owners in Arrears** | Multiple unit owners are not paying their service charges. The building company may need to take over those units, creating temporary financial strain for all other owners. | Check the arrears figure in the financial statements. Assess materiality. |

### 3.2 Technical / Physical Flags

| Flag | Description | Action |
|------|-------------|--------|
| 🔴 **Major Pipe / Services Renovation Overdue** | Buildings typically require full pipe and services renovation after 40–50 years. Cost is often €700–€1,200/m² (or local equivalent). If the building is at or past this age threshold and work is only listed as "under investigation," a major unplanned cost is likely imminent. | Confirm the building's construction year and the status of any planned services renovation. Model the cost to your unit. |
| 🔴 **Facade or Windows — No Recent Work** | In climates with significant weather exposure, if the exterior cladding or windows have not been addressed in 30+ years, a major renovation project is likely approaching. | Request the building's maintenance history and planned works schedule. |
| 🟠 **No EV Charging Infrastructure or Plan** | Buildings without electric vehicle (EV) charging or a credible plan to install it may become less attractive to premium tenants as EV adoption increases. A collective electrical panel upgrade can be expensive. | Assess relevance to your target tenant profile and local EV adoption rates. |

### 3.3 Legal Flags

| Flag | Description | Action |
|------|-------------|--------|
| 🔴 **Redemption / Pre-emption Clause** | As described in Section 1.2 — a clause in the building's constitution allowing the company or existing shareholders to acquire the unit at your purchase price after contracts are exchanged. | Confirm with the estate agent and solicitor. Avoid unless the deal is exceptional. |

### 3.4 Social / Community Flags

| Flag | Description | Action |
|------|-------------|--------|
| 🟠 **Very High Investor Ownership (>70%)** | A building dominated by investors often suffers from neglected common areas and a "minimise costs" approach to maintenance, reducing asset quality over time. | Assess the building's condition and maintenance history carefully. |
| 🟠 **Very Low Investor Ownership (<20%)** | An owner-occupier-dominated building may have a management board that is strict on tenant behaviour. This can create friction with your tenants and complaints about noise, smoking, or lifestyle. | Review board meeting minutes for any history of tenant-related disputes. |

## 4. After Buying

- **Landlord Insurance:**
  The building's insurance covers the structure. As a landlord, take out a separate policy covering:
  - Your personal liability
  - Loss of rental income
  - Contents you own (white goods, fixtures you are responsible for)
  - Typically tax-deductible as a rental expense.

- **Landlord Association Membership:**
  Consider joining a local landlord or property investors association. Membership typically provides access to standard lease templates, legal advice lines, and industry updates. Membership fees are generally tax-deductible.

## 5. Before Renting

### 5.1 Tenant Screening

- **Credit Check:** Run a formal credit check via an authorised credit reference agency. Confirm your legal basis for doing so under applicable data protection law. Always obtain the applicant's written consent.
- **Proof of Income:** Request recent payslips (typically 2–3 months) or an employment contract. The general guideline is that monthly rent should not exceed 30–40% of the applicant's gross monthly income.
- **References:** Request a reference from the applicant's previous landlord where possible.

### 5.2 The Lease Agreement

- **Lease Type:**
  - **Fixed-Term:** Both parties are bound for the full term. Early termination is difficult and may trigger financial penalties. Appropriate for tenants with known temporary needs or where you want predictable occupancy.
  - **Open-Ended (Rolling):** Continues until either party gives notice. More flexible; notice periods are set by local legislation.

- **Rent and Escalation:**
  State the rent amount clearly. Define the mechanism for increases — typically tied to an official consumer price or cost-of-living index, or a fixed annual percentage. Vague escalation clauses cause disputes.

- **Security Deposit:**
  Typically 1–3 months' rent. Confirm legal requirements for how it must be held (separate account, etc.) in your jurisdiction. Hand over keys only after the deposit has cleared.

- **Utilities:**
  Specify in the lease whether water is included (flat fee or metered), and that the tenant is responsible for arranging their own electricity contract. Ambiguity here leads to disputes.

- **Home/Contents Insurance Requirement:**
  Require the tenant to hold valid home/contents insurance for the duration of the lease. This protects both parties in the event of accidental damage.

### 5.3 Handover & Documentation

- **Condition Report:** Walk through the property with the tenant before handover. Photograph all rooms, surfaces, appliances, and any pre-existing damage. Both parties sign the condition report. This is your primary evidence in any deposit dispute.
- **Key Log:** Record precisely how many keys (and access cards/fobs) are handed over. State the cost of replacement if keys are not returned.
- **Appliance Manuals:** Provide operating manuals for all appliances. This prevents damage caused by misuse and reduces maintenance calls.
- **Notification to Building Management:** Inform the building manager or body corporate of the new occupant as required by local law or building rules.

## 6. Tax & Financial Administration

> **Note:** Tax rules vary by jurisdiction. The principles below reflect broadly applicable concepts. Always verify with a qualified tax adviser in your country of investment.

- **Rental Income Tax:**
  Rental income is typically taxed as capital income or investment income (not employment income), often at a flat or preferential rate. Confirm the applicable rate and any thresholds in your jurisdiction.

- **Deductible Expenses (commonly allowable):**
  Keep all receipts for:
  - Building service charges / maintenance fees
  - Capital charges where recorded as income by the building (see Section 1.1)
  - Loan interest on your investment mortgage
  - Landlord insurance premiums
  - Landlord association membership fees
  - Property management fees
  - Repairs and maintenance (not improvements — see below)
  - Advertising and tenant-finding costs

- **Capital Improvements vs. Repairs:**
  A repair restores the property to its prior condition (deductible in the year incurred). An improvement enhances the property beyond its prior standard (typically added to acquisition cost and deducted on sale via capital gains calculation). The line between the two is often a point of contention with tax authorities.

- **Pre-Tenancy Renovation Costs:**
  If you renovate a property before the first tenant moves in, those costs are generally added to the acquisition cost rather than being deductible from rental income. Seek specific advice on this point.

- **Record-Keeping:**
  Maintain a dedicated folder (physical or digital) per property containing:
  - Purchase documents and transfer taxes
  - All receipts and invoices
  - Lease agreements
  - Condition reports and handover documentation
  - Correspondence with tenants and building management
  - Annual financial statements from the building company

## 7. Renovation — Capital Charge Tax Treatment

This section elaborates on the critical tax distinction introduced in Section 1.1, as it has a direct impact on acquisition decisions and annual cash flow.

### 7.1 The Two Methods

When a building company collects capital charges from owners to repay a renovation loan, the accounting method they use determines your tax position:

| Method | Building records it as... | Your tax treatment |
|--------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| **Expensed / Income Method** | Revenue in the income statement | You deduct the full charge (principal + interest) from taxable rental income each year → immediate tax saving |
| **Capitalised / Funded Method** | Capital contribution on the balance sheet | Not deductible from annual income → adds to your acquisition cost → reduces capital gains tax only on eventual sale |

### 7.2 How to Verify Before Buying

1. **Request the Property Manager's Certificate** (or equivalent official building document). Look for the section describing how capital charges are treated. It should explicitly state whether charges are expensed or capitalised.
2. **Review Annual General Meeting Minutes.** The decision on treatment is often made at the AGM. Well-managed buildings often commit in their rules to expensing charges "whenever possible" to attract investor buyers.

### 7.3 Timing of Renovations

- **Renovation during tenancy:** If the building carries out a renovation while your unit is tenanted (or actively being marketed), and the charge is expensed, deduct it in the relevant tax year.
- **Renovation before first tenancy:** If you buy a vacant property and the building renovation happens before you find a tenant, the capital charge rules (expensed or capitalised) still apply to building-level charges. Your own personal renovation work carried out before the first tenancy typically cannot be deducted from rental income and must be added to acquisition cost.
